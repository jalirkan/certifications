CISA DECISION RULES - generated from principles.json
==============================================================================
These are the rules that generate answers across domains. When a question is
unfamiliar, work out which rule it turns on.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Risk assessment precedes control selection
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Controls are chosen because a risk assessment identified the exposure.
  Anything that selects controls from a catalog, a peer benchmark, or last
  year's findings has skipped the step that makes the selection defensible.

  WHY: Controls exist to reduce risk relative to business objectives. Without
  an assessment, the control set reflects someone else's assumptions and
  effort goes where it is not needed while real exposures stay open.

  TRAP: Reaching for the framework, the tool, or the training as the first
  step. All three are plausible and all three presuppose an answer to a
  question nobody has asked yet.

  NOT WHEN: Applies to selecting and prioritizing controls. It does not mean a
  risk assessment precedes every action - during an active incident,
  containment comes first.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. Prevent beats detect beats correct
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  When the stem asks what BEST addresses a weakness, the preventive control
  outranks the detective one, and detection outranks correction.

  WHY: A control that stops the event removes the loss entirely. Detection
  only shortens the exposure, and correction only limits what has already
  happened.

  TRAP: Choosing monitoring, logging, review or reporting when something could
  have stopped the event outright. These feel responsible and are usually the
  second-best answer.

  NOT WHEN: ONLY when the stem asks what addresses or prevents a weakness. If
  it asks about recovery, evidence, or what to do after an event, the ranking
  inverts and detective or corrective controls win.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. Accountability cannot be outsourced
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Tasks move to a provider; responsibility for the outcome does not. The
  organization remains answerable for its data, its compliance and its risk
  regardless of who performs the work.

  WHY: Regulators, customers and the board hold the organization to account,
  not its suppliers. That is why oversight, assurance rights and exit planning
  have to be designed into the arrangement.

  TRAP: Assuming a contract, a certification or a provider's own assurances
  transfer the obligation. They transfer the activity and, at most, some
  financial consequence.

  NOT WHEN: About accountability specifically. Operational tasks genuinely do
  transfer, and expecting the customer to patch a SaaS platform is the
  opposite error.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. Design evidence is not operating evidence
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Policies, approvals, charters, certifications and plans establish what
  should happen. Only records of the control running over a period establish
  that it did.

  WHY: A control can be perfectly designed and never performed. Operating
  effectiveness is a claim about a period of time and needs evidence covering
  that period.

  TRAP: Accepting an approved policy, a signed attestation, a certificate or a
  documented procedure as proof the control works. Each is real evidence - of
  design.

  NOT WHEN: When the stem asks for evidence of operating effectiveness. If it
  asks whether a control is appropriately designed, design evidence is exactly
  what you want.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. Completeness beats sampling
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The dangerous item is the one missing from your list. Reconcile the recorded
  population against an independent source before concluding anything about
  it.

  WHY: A sample drawn from an incomplete population tells you only about the
  part that was already known. No sample size compensates for records that
  were never there.

  TRAP: Treating a clean sample, a licence count, or a vendor console total as
  evidence of coverage. All three describe the managed population and are
  silent about what sits outside it.

  NOT WHEN: Strongest where the risk is unmanaged or unrecorded items. Not
  relevant when the population is closed and known.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. Contain before eradicate before recover
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  While something is actively spreading, stop the spread first. Investigation,
  attribution, restoration and notification all follow.

  WHY: Every minute of active propagation enlarges the loss. Containment
  converts an expanding problem into a fixed one that can then be worked
  methodically.

  TRAP: Restoring from backup (reinfects and destroys evidence), hunting for
  who caused it (belongs to later investigation), or notifying regulators
  (depends on a scope assessment you cannot yet make).

  NOT WHEN: Incident response specifically, and only while the incident is
  live. Preparation work belongs before, and lessons learned after.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. Never sample from a population the auditee controls
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Test from a population whose completeness you established independently, and
  work from the system of record toward the approvals rather than the reverse.

  WHY: A list produced by the party under review can omit exactly the records
  that would reveal the problem, and nothing in the sample will show that it
  did.

  TRAP: Accepting a system-generated report because it looks authoritative, or
  testing forward from approved requests, which can only ever confirm that
  approved things happened.

  NOT WHEN: Evidence gathering and sampling. Auditee-produced data is fine
  once its completeness and accuracy have been separately established.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. Address the cause, not the instance
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  A finding is resolved when the mechanism that produced it is removed.
  Correcting the specific exceptions found leaves the mechanism intact.

  WHY: Instance-level fixes guarantee the same finding reappears at the next
  audit, and the organization pays the cost repeatedly without ever buying the
  fix.

  TRAP: Accepting remediation of the sampled items, more training, or a policy
  reminder as the answer when the process itself produced the failure.

  NOT WHEN: Recommendations and problem management. During an active incident
  a workaround that restores service is correct even though it is not a cure.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
9. Business impact sets priority
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Criticality, recovery order and remediation sequence follow from consequence
  to the business, not from technical difficulty, cost, user count or who
  asked loudest.

  WHY: Only the business can quantify what an outage or a breach costs it.
  Priorities set inside IT track system complexity and visibility instead.

  TRAP: Ranking by number of users affected, by monetary value alone, by cost
  of the fix, or by how hard the work is.

  NOT WHEN: Prioritization decisions. Not a rule about who performs the work.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
10. Nobody marks their own homework
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Whoever performs an activity should not be the one who approves, reviews or
  reports on it. Whoever can grant access can use it.

  WHY: Self-review removes the independent check that makes the control
  meaningful, and concentrating authorization with execution lets one person
  complete a harmful action unaided.

  TRAP: Accepting review by the person's own manager within the same function,
  or treating logging as a substitute for separation. Detection after the fact
  is not segregation.

  NOT WHEN: Applies to control design and to auditor independence alike.
  Compensating controls are legitimate where segregation is genuinely
  impractical in a small team.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
11. Management decides, the auditor evaluates
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The auditor assesses, reports and escalates. Management owns the decision,
  accepts the risk, implements the control and answers for compliance.

  WHY: An auditor who implements or decides is auditing their own work at the
  next engagement, and the organization loses the independent view it created
  the function to get.

  TRAP: Having the auditor fix it, overrule management, remove a finding to
  keep the peace, or go straight to a regulator. All feel decisive; each
  destroys the role.

  NOT WHEN: About the division of responsibility. It does not stop the auditor
  recommending, advising early in a project, or escalating firmly to the audit
  committee.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
12. Authorization precedes action
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Before testing, changing, accessing or deploying, confirm that someone with
  the authority to permit it has done so, in writing, with scope defined.

  WHY: Without authorization the activity is indistinguishable from the attack
  or the unauthorized change it resembles, and there is no agreed boundary
  when something goes wrong.

  TRAP: Focusing on the competence of the people, the quality of the tools, or
  the technical safeguards instead of on whether permission exists.

  NOT WHEN: Strongest for penetration testing, production changes and access
  provisioning. Emergency procedures legitimately defer approval, but require
  retrospective review.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
13. Untested means unknown
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  A control, plan, backup or recovery capability that has never been exercised
  has unknown effectiveness, whatever its design says.

  WHY: The failure surfaces at the moment of need, which is the worst possible
  time to discover it. Testing converts an assumption into evidence.

  TRAP: Accepting approval, documentation, vendor assurance or the existence
  of the capability as evidence it works. Also accepting a partial test as a
  full one.

  NOT WHEN: Universal for controls that only run under stress: suppression
  systems, generators, backups, continuity plans, failover.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
14. A tool only sees what reaches it
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Monitoring, scanning, filtering and prevention are bounded by what is
  actually fed to them. Sources not forwarding, traffic not decrypted and
  assets not enrolled are invisible.

  WHY: Coverage gaps produce false confidence, which is more dangerous than a
  known absence of control because the risk is treated as managed.

  TRAP: Judging a tool by its configuration, its rules or its licence count
  rather than by what is in scope. Also assuming alerts generated equal alerts
  reviewed.

  NOT WHEN: Detection and prevention tooling. Not about tuning quality, which
  is a separate weakness.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
15. A control that acts too late has not acted
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Detection, review and escalation must happen inside the window in which they
  can change the outcome. Beyond that window the control provides evidence,
  not protection.

  WHY: Objectives with deadlines - overnight batch, breach notification,
  month-end close - are not met by a control operating on a monthly cycle.

  TRAP: Accepting periodic review or after-the-fact reporting as adequate when
  the stem sets an implicit deadline.

  NOT WHEN: Wherever the stem attaches a time constraint. A quarterly review
  is perfectly adequate for a risk that moves quarterly.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
16. Direct and independent evidence outranks representation
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Evidence the auditor obtains or produces beats evidence supplied by the
  party under review, which beats what that party says about it.

  WHY: Reliability rises with the auditor's proximity to the evidence and with
  the independence of its source. Inquiry is the weakest form and reports
  intent rather than behavior.

  TRAP: Relying on interviews, management representations, self-reported
  dashboards or vendor questionnaires because they are consistent and readily
  available.

  NOT WHEN: Evidence reliability. Inquiry remains valuable for building
  understanding and directing testing - just not for concluding.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
17. The verb governs, not the scenario
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The same situation has different correct answers depending on whether the
  stem asks for the greatest risk, the best control, the first step, or the
  best evidence. Answer the question actually asked.

  WHY: Distractors are built from correct answers to the neighbouring
  question. An option can be entirely true, and about this scenario, and still
  be wrong because it answers a different verb.

  TRAP: Recognizing the scenario, retrieving what you know about it, and
  selecting the most familiar true statement rather than the one responsive to
  the stem.

  NOT WHEN: Universal. This is the single most common way to lose marks on
  material you actually know.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
18. Measure the outcome, not the activity
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Effectiveness is demonstrated by what changed, not by what was performed.
  Completion rates, volumes produced and records opened describe effort; a
  measure only counts if it moves when the thing it claims to measure moves.

  WHY: Activity metrics are easy to collect and easy to satisfy without
  achieving anything, so they drift toward being gamed. Outcome measures
  resist that because the underlying reality has to change.

  TRAP: Accepting completion percentages, ticket counts, findings raised, or
  documents published as evidence a programme works.

  NOT WHEN: Judging effectiveness. Activity measures remain useful
  operationally for capacity and workload management.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
19. Diagnose before you prescribe
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  When something unexpected appears - an exception, a mass of alerts,
  widespread workarounds - establish what it means before responding to it.

  WHY: The obvious response usually treats a symptom, and acting immediately
  forecloses the finding. One exception may be isolated or systemic, and 4,000
  hits usually means the rule is wrong rather than the control.

  TRAP: Escalating, blocking, extending the sample or reporting the raw count
  before establishing what the observation actually indicates.

  NOT WHEN: Analysis and response design. It does not license delay during an
  active incident, where containment precedes diagnosis.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
20. Cost of correction rises with time
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  A defect, a control gap or a bad requirement costs least at the point it is
  introduced and most in production. Involvement early is worth more than
  thoroughness late.

  WHY: Everything built on top of an error has to be reworked with it. By
  production the cost includes business impact, emergency change and data
  correction.

  TRAP: Deferring control design, audit involvement or privacy assessment
  until the review stage, where the finding is real but the remedy is
  expensive or refused.

  NOT WHEN: Design and delivery lifecycles. Early involvement must stay
  advisory, or the auditor loses independence at the later review.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
21. Concentration is the exposure
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Where one person, one key, one supplier, one site or one component carries
  the whole capability, its loss is the organization's loss. Look for what
  everything silently depends on.

  WHY: Redundancy is usually partial. Duplicated servers share a switch; two
  data centres share a grid; a resilient team has one person who understands
  the legacy system.

  TRAP: Managing the symptom - paying the key person more, monitoring the
  supplier harder - instead of removing the dependency through cross-training,
  segmentation or a second source.

  NOT WHEN: Availability and continuity. Concentration is sometimes a
  deliberate and correct trade for consistency, as with centralized IT.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------
22. A decision nobody owns has not been made
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Exceptions, risk acceptances, go-live calls and sign-offs must be
  documented, made by someone with authority to make them, and time-bounded
  where relevant.

  WHY: Undocumented decisions cannot be reviewed, revisited or defended, and
  temporary accommodations quietly become permanent unmanaged risk.

  TRAP: Accepting informal approval, a plan to fix it later, or the absence of
  objection as though a decision had been taken.

  NOT WHEN: Governance of deviations and approvals. Not an argument for
  documenting every routine action.
